# Chapter 02: Symmetric Cryptography — Block Ciphers, SPN & AES Internals

> "A block cipher is the workhorse of modern cryptography. It is a family of pseudorandom permutations parameterized by a key, transforming fixed-size blocks of plaintext into blocks of ciphertext that are computationally indistinguishable from pure mathematical noise."  
> — *Jonathan Katz & Yehuda Lindell, Introduction to Modern Cryptography*

---

## ⚙️ 1. Block Cipher Foundations: PRFs, PRPs & Shannon's Principles

A **Block Cipher** is an invertible deterministic function:
$$E : \{0, 1\}^k \times \{0, 1\}^n \longrightarrow \{0, 1\}^n$$
where $k$ is the key length and $n$ is the block size. For every fixed key $K \in \{0, 1\}^k$, the function $E_K(x)$ is a bijection (permutation) over $\{0, 1\}^n$, with an efficient inverse decryption function $D_K(y) = E_K^{-1}(y)$.

### 1.1 Shannon's Two Pillars of Block Cipher Design
In 1949, Claude Shannon established that a secure block cipher must enforce two properties:
1. **Confusion:** Obscures the mathematical relationship between the secret key and the ciphertext bits. Typically achieved through non-linear Substitution Boxes (**S-boxes**).
2. **Diffusion:** Spreads the statistical influence of a single plaintext bit or key bit across as many ciphertext bits as possible (the **Avalanche Effect**). Flipping a single bit of plaintext must result in approximately $50\%$ of all ciphertext bits flipping randomly.

## 🔀 2. Architectural Paradigms: Feistel Networks vs. SPN

Modern block ciphers are engineered as **Iterated Ciphers**, executing multiple rounds of simple operations to produce complex non-linear security.

```text
===================================================================================================
                       FEISTEL NETWORKS VS. SUBSTITUTION-PERMUTATION NETWORKS
===================================================================================================

  [ Feistel Network (DES, 3DES, Blowfish) ]         [ Substitution-Permutation Network (AES) ]
  
  Input: Split into Left (L) and Right (R) halves   Input: Entire 128-bit block (State Matrix)
  
       L_{i-1}              R_{i-1}                              State Matrix
          │                    │                                       │
          │         ┌──────────┤                                       ▼
          │         │ Round Fxn│                                ┌──────────────┐
          │         │ F(R, K_i)│                                │   SubBytes   │ (Non-linear S-box)
          │         └─────┬────┘                                └──────┬───────┘
          │               │                                            │
          ▼               │                                            ▼
         XOR ◄────────────┘                                     ┌──────────────┐
          │                                                     │  ShiftRows   │ (Row Permutation)
          ▼                                                     └──────┬───────┘
       L_i = R_{i-1}      R_i = L_{i-1} ⊕ F(R, K_i)                    │
                                                                       ▼
  🌟 Major Advantage: Round Function F DOES NOT                ┌──────────────┐
     need to be invertible! Decryption is identical!            │  MixColumns  │ (Column Diffusion)
                                                                └──────┬───────┘
                                                                       │
                                                                       ▼
                                                                ┌──────────────┐
                                                                │ AddRoundKey  │ (XOR with K_i)
                                                                └──────────────┘
```

### 2.1 The Data Encryption Standard (DES) & Meet-in-the-Middle on 2DES
Standardized in 1977, DES used a 16-round Feistel network with a 64-bit block size and a **56-bit key**. 

By the late 1990s, custom FPGA hardware (EFF "Deep Crack") could brute-force the entire $2^{56}$ key space in under 24 hours.

#### Why Not Simply Double-Encrypt (2DES: $C = E_{K_2}(E_{K_1}(P))$)?
One might assume 2DES doubles security from 56 bits to $112\text{ bits}$.
However, the **Meet-in-the-Middle Attack** shatters 2DES in $2^{57}$ operations rather than $2^{112}$:
1. Compute and store in a hash table: $X = E_{K_1}(P)$ for all $2^{56}$ possible values of $K_1$.
2. For all $2^{56}$ possible values of $K_2$, compute $Y = D_{K_2}(C)$.
3. Check for a match in the hash table where $X = Y$!
Because of this attack, industry transitioned to **Triple-DES (3DES)**:
$$C = E_{K_3}(D_{K_2}(E_{K_1}(P)))$$
yielding an effective key length of 112 or 168 bits.

## 💎 3. The Advanced Encryption Standard (AES / FIPS 197)

Selected by NIST in 2001 after a global competition, **Rijndael** (designed by Belgian cryptographers Vincent Rijmen and Joan Daemen) became the worldwide **Advanced Encryption Standard (AES)**.

### 3.1 Parameters by Key Size
AES operates on a fixed **128-bit block size** ($16\text{ bytes}$) arranged as a $4 \times 4$ column-major byte matrix called the **State**:

$$\text{State Matrix} = \begin{bmatrix} s_{0,0} & s_{0,1} & s_{0,2} & s_{0,3} \\ s_{1,0} & s_{1,1} & s_{1,2} & s_{1,3} \\ s_{2,0} & s_{2,1} & s_{2,2} & s_{2,3} \\ s_{3,0} & s_{3,1} & s_{3,2} & s_{3,3} \end{bmatrix}$$

| Variant | Key Length ($k$) | Rounds ($N_r$) | Security Margin |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **AES-128** | 128 bits (16 bytes) | **10 rounds** | $2^{128}$ operations (immune to classical brute force) |
| **AES-192** | 192 bits (24 bytes) | **12 rounds** | $2^{192}$ operations |
| **AES-256** | 256 bits (32 bytes) | **14 rounds** | $2^{256}$ operations (Quantum resistant via Grover's algorithm) |

## 🔬 4. Step-by-Step Round Mathematics in AES

Every round of AES (except the final round, which omits `MixColumns`) executes four algebraic transformations:

```text
===================================================================================================
                                      AES ROUND TRANSFORMATIONS
===================================================================================================

  State In ──► [ 1. SubBytes ] ──► [ 2. ShiftRows ] ──► [ 3. MixColumns ] ──► [ 4. AddRoundKey ] ──► State Out
```

### 4.1 Step 1: `SubBytes` (Galois Field Non-Linear Inversion)
Provides pure **Confusion**. Each byte in the State matrix is independently replaced by its corresponding byte in the AES **S-box**.

The S-box is constructed from rigorous finite field mathematics:
1. Treat each byte as an element in the Galois Field $GF(2^8)$ defined by the irreducible polynomial:
   $$m(x) = x^8 + x^4 + x^3 + x + 1$$
2. Compute the multiplicative inverse $b^{-1}$ in $GF(2^8)$ (with $0^{-1} \equiv 0$).
3. Apply an affine transformation over $GF(2)$ to eliminate simple algebraic properties:
   $$b'_i = b_i \oplus b_{(i+4)\bmod 8} \oplus b_{(i+5)\bmod 8} \oplus b_{(i+6)\bmod 8} \oplus b_{(i+7)\bmod 8} \oplus c_i$$
   where $c = 0x63$.
This algebraic construction provably maximizes resistance against **Linear and Differential Cryptanalysis**.

---

### 4.2 Step 2: `ShiftRows` (Cyclic Permutation)
Provides inter-column **Diffusion**. The bytes in each row of the State matrix are cyclically shifted to the left by row index offsets:
- Row 0: No shift.
- Row 1: Shift left by **1 byte**.
- Row 2: Shift left by **2 bytes**.
- Row 3: Shift left by **3 bytes**.

$$\begin{bmatrix} s_{0,0} & s_{0,1} & s_{0,2} & s_{0,3} \\ s_{1,0} & s_{1,1} & s_{1,2} & s_{1,3} \\ s_{2,0} & s_{2,1} & s_{2,2} & s_{2,3} \\ s_{3,0} & s_{3,1} & s_{3,2} & s_{3,3} \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{\text{ShiftRows}} \begin{bmatrix} s_{0,0} & s_{0,1} & s_{0,2} & s_{0,3} \\ s_{1,1} & s_{1,2} & s_{1,3} & s_{1,0} \\ s_{2,2} & s_{2,3} & s_{2,0} & s_{2,1} \\ s_{3,3} & s_{3,0} & s_{3,1} & s_{3,2} \end{bmatrix}$$

---

### 4.3 Step 3: `MixColumns` (Matrix Multiplication over $GF(2^8)$)
Takes each column of 4 bytes and treats it as a polynomial over $GF(2^8)$, multiplying it by a fixed Maximum Distance Separable (MDS) matrix:

$$\begin{bmatrix} s'_{0,c} \\ s'_{1,c} \\ s'_{2,c} \\ s'_{3,c} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 02 & 03 & 01 & 01 \\ 01 & 02 & 03 & 01 \\ 01 & 01 & 02 & 03 \\ 03 & 01 & 01 & 02 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} s_{0,c} \\ s_{1,c} \\ s_{2,c} \\ s_{3,c} \end{bmatrix}$$

Because the branch metric of this MDS matrix is 5, modifying a single input byte in a column **guarantees that all 4 output bytes change**! Coupled with `ShiftRows`, two rounds of AES guarantee that a single bit change affects **every single bit of the entire 128-bit block**!

---

### 4.4 Step 4: `AddRoundKey`
The State matrix is XORed with the 128-bit round key derived from the master key via the **Rijndael Key Schedule**:
$$\text{State}' = \text{State} \oplus \text{RoundKey}_i$$

## ⚡ 5. Hardware Acceleration: Intel/AMD AES-NI

Software implementations of AES historically used precomputed 1 KB lookup tables (**T-tables**). In multi-tenant cloud servers, an attacker sharing the same physical CPU core could observe cache eviction timing and recover the secret key within minutes (**Cache-Timing Side-Channel Attacks**).

Modern CPUs (x86_64 and ARM64) incorporate dedicated silicon instructions:
- `AESENC` / `AESENCLAST`: Executes a full AES round in hardware in **a single clock cycle**.
- **Constant Time:** Silicon execution time is identical regardless of key or data values, completely eliminating cache-timing vulnerabilities and achieving throughput exceeding **$8\text{ Gigabytes/sec}$ per core**.

## 1. Intuition & 101 Motivation

While DeepSeek-R1-Zero proved that reasoning capabilities can emerge purely from reinforcement learning without human demonstrations, the resulting model had severe practical limitations:
1. **Poor Readability & Formatting:** Thoughts were stream-of-consciousness, frequently lacking paragraph breaks or clean mathematical notation.
2. **Language Mixing:** The model constantly switched languages mid-sentence (e.g. alternating between English and Chinese).
3. **Severe General Domain Degradation:** Optimizing purely for mathematical correctness degraded the model's performance on standard conversational tasks, creative writing, and translation.

To transform raw emergent reasoning into a polished, production-grade foundation model, the full **DeepSeek-R1 pipeline** developed a sophisticated **4-Stage Training Architecture**:
1. **Cold-Start SFT:** Pre-conditioning the base model with thousands of carefully curated, readable Chain-of-Thought examples.
2. **Large-Scale Reasoning RL:** GRPO on mathematical, coding, and scientific benchmarks.
3. **Rejection Sampling & General SFT:** Synthesizing 600K high-quality reasoning traces and combining them with 200K general conversational demonstrations.
4. **Secondary Multi-Domain RL:** Final reinforcement learning balancing mathematical accuracy with human aesthetic preferences and safety.

The second monumental breakthrough of the R1 project was **Reasoning Distillation**:
> *Can small, deployable models (1.5B, 7B, 14B, 32B) achieve frontier reasoning performance without undertaking expensive, multi-month RL training?*

DeepSeek proved that fine-tuning small dense models (e.g. Qwen2.5, LLaMA-3) directly on 800,000 curated reasoning traces generated by the 671B teacher produces small models that drastically outperform small models trained with RL from scratch!


## 2. Rigorous Mathematical Formulation

### 2.1 The 4-Stage DeepSeek-R1 Architecture

#### Stage 1: Cold-Start Data Curation
To prevent the early training chaos and language-mixing seen in R1-Zero, a small high-quality seed dataset $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cold}}$ ($\approx 5,000$ examples) is constructed:
- Few-shot prompting with long CoT examples.
- Post-processing to enforce markdown readability and strict language consistency.
- Fine-tune base model $\theta_{\text{base}}$ to obtain $\theta_{\text{cold}}$.

#### Stage 2: Large-Scale Reasoning RL (GRPO)
Train $\theta_{\text{cold}}$ using GRPO with rule-based deterministic verifiers (accuracy reward $R_{\text{acc}}$ + format reward $R_{\text{fmt}}$) until convergence on competition mathematics and algorithmic coding.

#### Stage 3: Rejection Sampling & Multi-Domain SFT
Using the checkpoint $\theta_{\text{RL}}$ from Stage 2:
1. For each reasoning problem, sample $N_{\text{sample}} = 32$ trajectories.
2. Filter through rule-based verifiers, retaining only correct answers.
3. Remove unreadable or language-mixed traces.
4. Curate 600,000 verified reasoning trajectories ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{reasoning}}$).
5. Mix with 200,000 general-domain instruction pairs ($\mathcal{D}_{\text{general}}$) covering writing, roleplay, translation, and factual QA.
6. Train the base model $\theta_{\text{base}}$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{stage3}} = \mathcal{D}_{\text{reasoning}} \cup \mathcal{D}_{\text{general}}$.

#### Stage 4: Secondary Multi-Domain RL
Train the Stage 3 checkpoint using combined rewards:
$$R_{\text{stage4}} = \begin{cases} R_{\text{accuracy}} + R_{\text{format}} & \text{if } q \in \text{Reasoning Domain} \\ R_{\text{preference}}(\text{Helpfulness, Harmlessness}) & \text{if } q \in \text{General Domain} \end{cases}$$

### 2.2 Knowledge Distillation: Token-Level vs. Sequence-Level

How does the knowledge of the 671B MoE teacher model $\pi_T$ transfer to a compact student model $\pi_S$?

#### 1. Token-Level Knowledge Distillation (Hinton et al., 2015):
The student is trained to minimize the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the full vocabulary probability distributions:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{Token-KD}}(\theta_S) = \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\left( \pi_T(\cdot \mid x) \,\|\, \pi_S(\cdot \mid x) \right) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \pi_T(v \mid x) \log \left( \frac{\pi_T(v \mid x)}{\pi_S(v \mid x)} \right)$$

*Engineering Bottleneck:* Token-level KD requires running forward passes on both the 671B teacher and the student simultaneously, demanding massive GPU clusters during student training.

#### 2. Sequence-Level Knowledge Distillation (Kim & Rush, 2016):
In sequence-level KD, the teacher generates entire trajectories $y_T = (z_T, \text{ans}_T)$ offline via rejection sampling.
The student is trained using standard supervised cross-entropy directly on the teacher's sampled tokens:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{Seq-KD}}(\theta_S) = - \sum_{t=1}^{|y_T|} \log \pi_S(y_{T, t} \mid x, y_{T, <t})$$

This completely decouples student training from teacher inference!

### 2.3 Theorem: The Small-Model Exploration Trap

Why does distillation outperform training small models with RL from scratch?

#### Theorem (The Zero-Advantage Cold-Start Trap):
Consider training a small student model $\pi_S$ with GRPO on a competition math problem $q$.
Let $p_S(q)$ be the probability that $\pi_S$ generates a correct trajectory on a single attempt.
For difficult Olympiad problems (e.g. AIME), a 1.5B or 7B base model has $p_S(q) \approx 0.001$ (1 in 1,000).

In GRPO with group size $G = 8$:
The probability that **all 8 rollouts fail** ($R_i = 0$ for all $i \in \{1, \dots, 8\}$) is:
$$P(\text{All Fail}) = (1 - 0.001)^8 \approx 0.9920 \quad (99.2\%)$$

When all rollouts receive reward $0.0$:
$$\mu_R = 0, \quad \sigma_R = 0 \implies A_i = \frac{0 - 0}{0 + \epsilon} = 0 \quad \forall i$$
The policy gradient is **identically zero**:
$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}} = \mathbf{0}$$

**Conclusion:** Small models cannot learn frontier reasoning via RL from scratch because they never stumble upon the correct solution to receive a reward signal! Distillation provides the student with verified, pre-paved reasoning highways, bypassing the exploration trap entirely. $\blacksquare$

### 5.3 Step-by-Step Hand Calculations

#### Part A: Sequence-Level Distillation Loss (Hard Target = Token 42)

1. **Compute Exponentials of Student Logits:**
   $$e^{H_{S, 1}} = e^{2.0} \approx \mathbf{7.389056}$$
   $$e^{H_{S, 2}} = e^{0.5} \approx \mathbf{1.648721}$$
   $$e^{H_{S, 3}} = e^{-1.0} \approx \mathbf{0.367879}$$
   $$\sum = 7.389056 + 1.648721 + 0.367879 = \mathbf{9.405656}$$

2. **Compute Softmax Probabilities:**
   $$P_S(41) = \frac{7.389056}{9.405656} \approx \mathbf{0.785597}$$
   $$P_S(42) = \frac{1.648721}{9.405656} \approx \mathbf{0.175290}$$
   $$P_S(43) = \frac{0.367879}{9.405656} \approx \mathbf{0.039113}$$

3. **Compute Cross-Entropy Loss on Target Token 42:**
   $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{seq}} = -\ln(P_S(42)) = -\ln(0.175290) \approx -(-1.741311) = \mathbf{1.741311}$$

---

#### Part B: Soft-Target Knowledge Distillation with $T_{\text{temp}} = 2.0$

1. **Scale Student Logits by $T_{\text{temp}} = 2.0$:**
   $$H_S^{\text{scaled}} = \left[ \frac{2.0}{2}, \frac{0.5}{2}, \frac{-1.0}{2} \right] = [1.000000, 0.250000, -0.500000]$$

2. **Compute Soft Student Exponentials:**
   $$e^{1.0} \approx \mathbf{2.718282}$$
   $$e^{0.25} \approx \mathbf{1.284025}$$
   $$e^{-0.5} \approx \mathbf{0.606531}$$
   $$\sum_{\text{soft}} = 2.718282 + 1.284025 + 0.606531 = \mathbf{4.608838}$$

3. **Compute Soft Student Probabilities $Q_S$:**
   $$Q_S(41) = \frac{2.718282}{4.608838} \approx \mathbf{0.589798}$$
   $$Q_S(42) = \frac{1.284025}{4.608838} \approx \mathbf{0.278601}$$
   $$Q_S(43) = \frac{0.606531}{4.608838} \approx \mathbf{0.131602}$$

4. **Compute KL Divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(P_T \,\|\, Q_S) = \sum_{k} P_T(k) \ln\left( \frac{P_T(k)}{Q_S(k)} \right)$:**
   - Component 1: $0.10 \ln\left(\frac{0.10}{0.589798}\right) \approx \mathbf{-0.177465}$
   - Component 2: $0.80 \ln\left(\frac{0.80}{0.278601}\right) \approx \mathbf{+0.843869}$
   - Component 3: $0.10 \ln\left(\frac{0.10}{0.131602}\right) \approx \mathbf{-0.027460}$

   $$\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}} = \mathbf{0.638944}$$

5. **Total Scaled KD Loss ($T^2 \cdot \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}$):**
   $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}} = 2.0^2 \times 0.638944 = 4.0 \times 0.638944 = \mathbf{2.555776}$$

---

#### Part C: Soft-Target Knowledge Distillation without Temperature Scaling ($T_{\text{temp}} = 1.0$)

1. **Unscaled Soft Student Probabilities $P_S$:**
   $P_S = [0.785597, 0.175290, 0.039113]$

2. **Compute KL Divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(P_T \,\|\, P_S)$:**
   - Component 1: $0.10 \ln\left(\frac{0.10}{0.785597}\right) = 0.10 \times (-2.061298) \approx \mathbf{-0.206130}$
   - Component 2: $0.80 \ln\left(\frac{0.80}{0.175290}\right) = 0.80 \times (1.518155) \approx \mathbf{+1.214524}$

3. **Total $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}$:**

## 6. Solved Illustrations

### Illustration 1: Why Small Models Fail at Cold-Start Exploration
**Problem:**
A 1.5B model has a per-token reasoning accuracy of $90\%$ ($p_{\text{token}} = 0.90$).
A math derivation requires a sequence of $L = 50$ consecutive reasoning tokens to reach the correct answer.
1. What is the probability that the model generates the entire derivation without a single mistake?
2. In a GRPO training step with group size $G = 16$, what is the probability that at least one rollout succeeds?

**Solution:**
1. **Full Sequence Success Probability:**
   $$P_{\text{success}} = (p_{\text{token}})^L = (0.90)^{50} \approx \mathbf{0.005154 \quad (0.515\%)}$$
2. **Probability of At Least One Success in Group $G = 16$:**
   $$P(\ge 1 \text{ success}) = 1 - (1 - 0.005154)^{16} = 1 - (0.994846)^{16} \approx 1 - 0.9206 = \mathbf{0.0794 \quad (7.94\%)}$$
*(Analysis: In over **92% of training batches**, every single rollout fails! GRPO receives zero advantage signal across 92% of steps, causing the small model to collapse or stall completely).* $\blacksquare$

### Illustration 2: KL Divergence Distillation Loss Computation

**Problem:**
Compute the token-level KL divergence distillation loss for a student model predicting a vocabulary of $V=5$ tokens.
Given:
- Teacher probability distribution: $P = [0.70, 0.15, 0.08, 0.05, 0.02]$
- Student probability distribution: $Q = [0.50, 0.25, 0.12, 0.08, 0.05]$

Compute $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}(P || Q)$ and compare it to the standard SFT hard-label cross-entropy loss (where the label is the teacher's argmax token).

**Step-by-Step Solution:**

**1. Compute KL Divergence $\sum P_i \log(P_i / Q_i)$:**
- **Term 0:** $0.70 \times \log(0.70 / 0.50) = 0.70 \times \log(1.40) = 0.70 \times 0.336472 = \mathbf{0.235530}$
- **Term 1:** $0.15 \times \log(0.15 / 0.25) = 0.15 \times \log(0.60) = 0.15 \times (-0.510826) = \mathbf{-0.076624}$
- **Term 2:** $0.08 \times \log(0.08 / 0.12) = 0.08 \times \log(0.6667) = 0.08 \times (-0.405465) = \mathbf{-0.032437}$
- **Term 3:** $0.05 \times \log(0.05 / 0.08) = 0.05 \times \log(0.625) = 0.05 \times (-0.470004) = \mathbf{-0.023500}$
- **Term 4:** $0.02 \times \log(0.02 / 0.05) = 0.02 \times \log(0.40) = 0.02 \times (-0.916291) = \mathbf{-0.018326}$

Total KL $= 0.235530 - 0.076624 - 0.032437 - 0.023500 - 0.018326 = \mathbf{0.084643}$ nats.

**2. Compute SFT Hard-Label Loss:**
- The teacher's highest probability token is index 0 ($0.70$).
- Hard-label cross-entropy $= -\log(Q[\text{argmax} P]) = -\log(Q[0]) = -\log(0.50) = \mathbf{0.693147}$ nats.

**Conclusion:**
The KL distillation loss ($0.0846$ nats) is over 8 times smaller than the hard-label SFT loss ($0.6931$ nats). Distillation preserves the soft structural distribution of the teacher, penalizing the student far less harshly and allowing for more efficient parameter updates. $\blacksquare$

### Illustration 3: Cold-Start Curriculum Expected Chain Length

**Problem:**
Track the expansion of reasoning length across the DeepSeek-R1 multi-stage curriculum. 
- Stage 1 (Cold-Start): 1,000 examples, mean reasoning tokens $= 500$.
- Stage 2 (GRPO Iteration 1): 10,000 prompts, mean reasoning tokens $= 850$.
- Stage 3 (GRPO Iteration 5): 10,000 prompts, mean reasoning tokens $= 1,800$.
- Stage 4 (Final GRPO): 20,000 prompts, mean reasoning tokens $= 3,200$.

Compute the total reasoning tokens consumed (the "curriculum budget").

**Step-by-Step Solution:**

1. **Stage 1 Total Tokens:**
   $$1,000 \text{ examples} \times 500 \text{ tokens/example} = \mathbf{500,000} \text{ tokens}$$
2. **Stage 2 Total Tokens:**
   $$10,000 \text{ examples} \times 850 \text{ tokens/example} = \mathbf{8,500,000} \text{ tokens}$$
3. **Stage 3 Total Tokens:**
   $$10,000 \text{ examples} \times 1,800 \text{ tokens/example} = \mathbf{18,000,000} \text{ tokens}$$
4. **Stage 4 Total Tokens:**
   $$20,000 \text{ examples} \times 3,200 \text{ tokens/example} = \mathbf{64,000,000} \text{ tokens}$$

**Total Curriculum Budget:**
$$\text{Total} = 0.5\text{M} + 8.5\text{M} + 18.0\text{M} + 64.0\text{M} = \mathbf{91,000,000} \text{ reasoning tokens}$$

This progression demonstrates how formatting rewards initially push toward structure (Stage 2), before the model independently discovers that extended reflection and verification chains (Stage 4) maximize success, reaching an "aha moment" behavior at 3,200 tokens per prompt. $\blacksquare$

### Illustration 4: Rejection Sampling Distillation Filtering Rate

**Problem:**
A massive 671B teacher model achieves $79.8\%$ pass@1 on the AIME 2024 benchmark. We wish to distill this capability into a 7B student model via rejection sampling.
- We generate $N = 32$ rollouts per problem and keep ONLY correct solutions.
- The dataset consists of 1,000 math problems.

Compute the expected yield of correct training pairs and analyze the compute-efficiency tradeoff if the 7B student subsequently achieves a $55.5\%$ pass@1 rate on the same benchmark.

**Step-by-Step Solution:**

**1. Calculate Yield Per Problem:**
Expected correct rollouts per problem $= N \times \text{pass@1}$
$$32 \times 0.798 = \mathbf{25.536}$$ correct solutions per problem.

**2. Calculate Total Yield Across Dataset:**
- Total generated rollouts $= 1,000 \times 32 = \mathbf{32,000}$ inference calls.
- Total expected correct rollouts kept $= 1,000 \times 25.536 = \mathbf{25,536}$ training pairs.

**3. Compute-Efficiency ROI Analysis:**
- We expended $32,000$ costly inference calls on the 671B teacher to synthesize the dataset.
- The student (7B) is trained on the $25,536$ verified $(s, r_{\text{correct}}, a_{\text{correct}})$ pairs.
- **ROI Result:** The resulting 7B student achieves $55.5\%$ accuracy. Running the 7B model costs roughly $8 \times$ to $10 \times$ less memory and compute than the 671B model. The rejection sampling dataset acts as a high-density knowledge transfer, giving edge-deployable models frontier-level math reasoning without any reinforcement learning. $\blacksquare$
